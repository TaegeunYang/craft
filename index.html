Abstract

Vision-language-action (VLA) models often struggle to generalize to skill combinations absent from their fine-tuning demonstrations, even when every constituent skill has been demonstrated. We focus on a vision shortcut as one failure mode: during fine-tuning, visual observations can serve as a proxy for the instruction, so a policy may execute a demonstrated combination associated with similar observations rather than the instructed combination. This motivates training with counterfactual pairs that hold a demonstration observation fixed while changing the instruction to specify an undemonstrated combination. These pairs, however, lack demonstrated actions for supervision. Crucially, the currently required skill has already been demonstrated, but actions from those executions cannot be transferred directly because the same skill can require different actions across observations. We propose CRAFT, which transfers supervision from demonstrated executions of the required skill to counterfactual pairs through reusable skill representations. Across three VLA models and two simulation benchmarks, CRAFT improves success on undemonstrated combinations while maintaining high success on demonstrated ones. CRAFT also improves compositional generalization on a real robot.

Motivation

Fine-tuned VLAs struggle with new skill combinations

We evaluate publicly released LIBERO-Goal checkpoints of four VLAs without further training. On five object–target combinations built from the suite’s own pick-and-place skills, success drops sharply, even though every constituent skill appears in a demonstrated task.

When these rollouts complete a task other than the instructed one, it is almost always a demonstrated task that shares the object or the target with the instruction. This behavior is consistent with a vision shortcut: during fine-tuning, the visual observation can serve as a proxy for the instruction.

Method

CRAFT: Counterfactual Skill-Representation Alignment for Fine-Tuning

We form counterfactual pairs by holding a demonstration observation fixed and changing the instruction to specify an undemonstrated combination. A pair has no demonstrated action, but the skill it currently requires has been demonstrated elsewhere. Because the same skill can require different actions under different observations, CRAFT does not copy those actions: it transfers supervision through reusable skill representations.

01

Skill and state representations

Learnable skill queries attend to image and instruction tokens and produce a skill representation that reflects the currently required skill. State queries attend only to image tokens and produce a state representation that captures visual information. The action expert is conditioned on both.

02 · ℒskill

Reusable skill representations

Holding the state representation, noisy action chunk, flow time, and target fixed, we swap in skill representations from other executions. A contrastive objective over the resulting prediction errors makes representations reusable across executions of the same skill while keeping different skills distinguishable.

03 · ℒcf

Counterfactual supervision

If the counterfactual changes the currently required skill, its skill representation is paired with the state representation of a demonstrated execution of that skill and supervised with that execution’s target velocity. If only future entities change, the original sample provides the target.

Benchmarks

Two compositional benchmarks

Built on LIBERO. In both, demonstrations cover every constituent skill but only same-color combinations; the remaining combinations are evaluated. We collect 50 demonstrations per demonstrated combination and will release both benchmarks and their demonstration datasets.

Results

Generalizing to undemonstrated combinations

Standard fine-tuning reaches high success on demonstrated combinations but much lower success on undemonstrated ones, whether the VLM is fully fine-tuned, adapted with LoRA, or frozen. CRAFT achieves the highest undemonstrated success across all three VLAs and both benchmarks while maintaining high success on demonstrated combinations.

Analysis

What does CRAFT learn?

It responds to instruction changes during execution

When an instruction change leaves the currently required skill unchanged, the policy keeps executing that skill. When the required skill changes, it begins executing the newly required skill, even from a state reached while executing a different one.

Instruction-change video coming soon

Its action predictions follow the instruction

Holding the visual observation fixed and varying only the instructed cube or plate, CRAFT’s predicted trajectories separate according to the instructed entity. Predictions from FT (Full) largely overlap across instructions.

Skill representations are organized by skill

Without ℒskill, representations from different entities are mixed within the pick and place groups. With ℒskill, they form entity-specific groups within each operation.

…and reusable across executions

Replacing a recipient’s skill representation with one from another execution of the same skill changes the predicted action chunk by only 4.0 ± 3.1% on average. Substitutions from different skills produce substantially larger changes.

Both counterfactual terms matter

ℒskill alone does not improve undemonstrated combinations. Adding ℒchange helps, and combining ℒchange with ℒpreserve yields by far the highest undemonstrated success while maintaining demonstrated success.

Real robot

Compositional generalization on a real robot

A Piper 6-DoF arm performs Pick-Place with four colored cubes and four colored plates, using third-person and wrist cameras. The demonstrations contain 20 episodes for each of the four same-color combinations; the other 12 combinations are undemonstrated. Both methods fine-tune π0.5 on the same data and are evaluated over five trials per combination.
